# Market Research Tools for Indie Makers

Your idea survived the [Discover stage](https://indiefa.st/blog/discover). Now comes the part most founders get wrong: they open a competitor's website, read the feature list, and call that research. It isn't. Competitor features tell you what someone built — they tell you nothing about how real customers described the problem before they found that product. That gap — between the language on a product page and the language in a customer's head — is where bad positioning, wrong feature bets, and missed markets come from. Actual market research for indie makers means finding the words customers use naturally, not the words a competitor's marketing team put on a homepage.

---

## Stop Researching Features. Start Researching Language.

Studying feature lists is the default research move because it feels productive. You open five competitor tabs, note what they offer, build a rough mental map of the market. The problem: that's a map of existing solutions, not a map of unsolved problems.

The research that actually moves things forward lives somewhere else. Reddit threads where people vent about a tool failing them mid-workflow. App store reviews where users describe what they wished the product did. Niche community posts where someone asks "does this tool exist?" and gets no satisfying answer. That's customer language — specific, unfiltered, and full of the exact phrases your future landing page should use.

When a founder does competitor analysis by reading feature lists, they end up building a slightly different version of what already exists. When they do Reddit research and review mining instead, they find the gap between what people say they need and what's currently on offer.

A good market research tool for indie makers surfaces this kind of language — not just search volume, not just TAM estimates. Real words, from real users, describing a real problem they haven't found a good solution to yet.

---

## What a Research Tool Actually Needs to Offer at This Stage

You are almost certainly pre-revenue here. That matters when evaluating tools. A research stack that costs $300/month makes sense for a funded team validating at scale. It doesn't make sense for a solo founder with an idea and a weekend.

What a research tool should give you at this stage:

- **Real customer language** — Reddit research, review mining, or community scraping. Not AI-synthesised summaries; actual user phrasing.
- **Keyword data with search volume and trend direction** — to check whether people are actively searching for the solution, not just the problem.
- **A usable free tier** — you should be able to do meaningful work before putting in a credit card. If you can't, it's built for a later stage.
- **Niche-level resolution** — not "project management software" but "project management for solo freelancers billing under $10K/month". That's the level where indie hacker research stack decisions actually get made.

The goal isn't to build a comprehensive research infrastructure. It's to answer one question before you commit to building: are real people actively expressing this problem in their own words, and is that demand growing?

Once your research holds up, the [Validate stage](https://indiefa.st/blog/validate) is where you test whether people will actually pay — not just whether the problem exists.

---

## The Scope Trap: You're Researching a Market When You Need a Niche

The most common research mistake isn't doing too little. It's researching at the wrong level.

"Project management tools" is a market. "Project management for solo freelance designers who bill by project, not by hour" is a niche. Those are not the same research problem. Most generic tools surface data at the market level, which is exactly where you don't want to be as an indie maker with no marketing budget.

When you research at market level, everything looks contested. When you scope to a niche, the picture changes: specific subreddits, specific long-tail keywords, specific review complaints about mainstream tools not fitting a smaller workflow. That's where the real niche research happens.

Niche scoping also makes demand validation concrete without needing to estimate a TAM. A total addressable market calculation is a story you tell investors. Twelve thousand monthly searches for a specific query with no strong result is evidence you can act on today.

Scope your research to the smallest version of your market that still has real, measurable demand. Validate from there. Expand later if it works.
