# MVP Validation Tools for Indie Hackers

Startup failure analyses — CB Insights among them — consistently find that building something nobody wanted is the single leading cause of startup death, accounting for roughly 42% of failures. Nearly half of everything built gets built wrong from the start. Not wrong technically. Wrong directionally: nobody asked for it, nobody paid for it, and the builder found out after months of work. Validation is not an optional pre-step you do when you have time. It's the only way to know, before committing to a build, whether you're solving a real problem or a hypothetical one. This page covers what actual MVP validation looks like — and which one tool IndieFast picked to run it fast.

---

## Validation Theater vs Real Validation

Most founders validate by telling people about their idea and watching how they react. That is not validation. That's social feedback — and social feedback lies.

A friend says "that's a great idea." A Twitter thread gets forty likes. Someone in a Discord server says "I'd definitely use that." None of this is data. People are kind. People say yes to avoid confrontation. People overestimate how much they want things when there's no cost attached to expressing interest.

Real validation has one definition: someone does something that costs them. They give you their email address before the product exists. They pay a pre-sale deposit. They click a button for a feature that isn't built yet — a fake door test — and you measure how many did. They say yes when directly asked if they'd pay, right now, at a specific price.

A waitlist with 200 signups from cold traffic is closer to real validation than a hundred encouraging replies. Someone handing you $50 before you've built anything is the real thing.

The goal of this stage is not reactions. It's willingness to pay, expressed through action. Anything that doesn't produce that is not validation — it's encouragement, and encouragement doesn't tell you whether you have a business.

## What a Validation Tool Actually Needs to Do

At this stage, you need to get something in front of real people fast enough that the test costs days, not months. A good idea validation tool for indie hackers should let you:

- **Spin up a landing page test in under an hour**, without writing code
- **Collect emails or pre-payments** against a value proposition that doesn't exist yet
- **Run a fake door test** to measure genuine click intent on a specific feature
- **Show conversion data**, not just visitor counts

The two things that matter most are speed and friction. If the tool takes three days to configure before you can run your first smoke test, it's built for a different kind of company. And if it can only gather passive "interest" without handling pre-payments or real waitlist signups, it's closer to a survey tool than a validation tool. The distinction matters.

Before committing to anything paid, check whether the free tier lets you run at least one real test. At this stage you're almost certainly pre-revenue, which means pre-budget. The tool that costs nothing to test your first hypothesis is the right starting point.

If you haven't completed the [Research stage](https://indiefa.st/blog/research) first — where you confirm real customer language and demand signal exist — you risk running validation on the wrong hypothesis. Come to validation with a focused problem statement, not a general direction.

## Stop Building Prototypes. Start Testing Hypotheses.

The most common mistake at the Validate stage: building a prototype when you should be running a test.

A prototype answers "can I build this?" That's an engineering question. Useful eventually — but not the question this stage is trying to answer. Validation answers a completely different question: "will anyone pay for this?" These require different tools, different timelines, and different outputs.

Founders who build prototypes at this stage are optimizing for the wrong signal. They show five people a working demo, get positive reactions, and interpret that as validation. It isn't. A prototype with zero pre-sales or waitlist signups is not a validated idea. It's a portfolio piece.

The test you need is faster than a prototype and cheaper than a full build. A landing page that states the value proposition clearly and has a real payment or signup action tells you more about product-market fit potential than two weeks of coding. If nobody converts, you haven't wasted a build cycle. If people do convert, you have genuine signal — not reactions, not feature requests, not encouragement. Signal you can build on.

Once validation holds, the [Plan stage](https://indiefa.st/blog/plan) is where that signal becomes a build roadmap.
